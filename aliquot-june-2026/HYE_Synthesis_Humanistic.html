This is an aliquot. In a lab, an aliquot is a small, measured portion drawn from a larger sample and studied as if it represents the whole. This site is one such portion of a living project, taken at a single moment and examined. It is not the project itself.

Everything here was assembled from a June 2026 archive of the Hello You Experiment on X, spanning the posts from October 2025 through June 2026, together with the public artifacts those posts reference. That corpus was run through a deliberately unoptimized AI analysis to map patterns, synthesize results, and interpret the project. The writing has not been heavily edited.

Read it as a snapshot dated June 2026, not as fact. It will contain mistakes, and it describes ideas and framings that were relevant when the posts were written, some of which the author may no longer hold or agree with. Its purpose is not to be correct but to fix the project at one stage so its evolution can be seen, and a later version measured against it. In the project's own language, this is one phenotype of the material, never the genotype.

The Synthesis

What happens to a mind the system stops hearing

A designer spent a year inside a workplace that separated what was said from what was true. Then he rebuilt that year, on purpose, as a public experiment. Here is what it reveals about meaning, work, and being seen.
A reading of the Hello You Experiment, drawn from its public record.
Roughly a 9 minute read.

There is a scene the author keeps returning to in his own writing. He is on a video call, camera on, and he is rocking gently in his chair with his eyes closed, the words arriving a half second late, like his mouth is waiting for his brain to finish the sentence. From the outside it looks like a glitch. From the inside it feels almost like an upgrade, a mind running hot because it is holding several versions of reality at once and none of them point the same way.

It hit a nerve for me, because most of us have felt a smaller version of that. The meeting where you already know the plan will change again. The Slack ping that lands in your stomach before your eyes. The quiet decision to stop saying the true thing because you have learned, through enough repetition, exactly what happens to that kind of honesty.

The Hello You Experiment is one person's attempt to take that feeling seriously enough to study it. He trained as a scientist first, in biochemistry, genetics, and neuroscience, then spent years as a designer, the last two and a half of them leading design at a profitable software company. He left in July 2025, during a leadership transition. Three months later he ran an experiment on himself, and the record of it is now scattered across a book, a film, a research paper, and hundreds of small posts. This is an attempt to read that record as one thing.

01The environment trains your nervous system

Biologists have a plain way of describing what an environment does to the things living inside it. It selects. Traits that help you survive get carried forward, traits that cost you get quietly removed, and after enough cycles the population looks the way the environment shaped it to look.

A workplace is an environment like this, and the author's sharpest claim is that we underrate how literally that works. When leadership narratives, priorities, and information all shift faster than anyone can reconcile them, people compensate by stitching fragments into a private model of reality that is true enough to act on. Do that long enough and cognition starts to mirror the structure of the place. Fragmented inputs produce fragmented thinking. Shifting incentives produce constant recalibration. He describes a version of professionalism that is really a high functioning way of holding contradictions without naming them, and he thinks we have started treating that as normal.

The numbers around him are not gentle. In Gallup's 2026 read on the global workplace, employee engagement sits at its lowest since 2020, and the managers who are supposed to absorb the chaos before it reaches their teams are now no better off than the people they lead.

The mechanism underneath the numbers is old. In the 1950s, the anthropologist Gregory Bateson and his colleagues described the double bind, a situation where a person receives contradictory demands and is also, quietly, forbidden from pointing out the contradiction. Bateson was studying families. The author's point is that the communication structure he named has simply moved into the modern org chart, and that the damage does not come from the contradiction itself. It comes from the rule against naming it out loud.

02The four days that did not end

Here is where the project stops being an essay and becomes an experiment. In October 2025, with his family away for four days, the author decided to find out whether the mental state he had developed at work would come back if he rebuilt the conditions that produced it.

So he did the thing a designer does. He treated the old workplace as a product experience and mapped it, with user journeys and flows and defined endpoints, except the user was himself. Siloed information became deliberately separated channels of his own notes. Shifting priorities became a rule against rereading old work while momentum held. He generated distorted noise on a guitar rig and trained himself to pull signal out of it, which left his mind in an alert, over associative state even after the noise stopped. He removed the finish lines inside the work, no deliverables, no milestones, no definition of done, because finish lines reduce ambiguity and he wanted more of it, not less.

It worked, and it worked past the point he intended. The four days opened into a long stretch he calls recombination, where associations raced faster than he could write them down and memory dissolved if it was not offloaded immediately. The part he was actually testing held. The state did not keep office hours. It followed him home and sat at the dinner table, because a mind that has learned to run this way does not come with an automatic off switch.

It also did not end on a schedule. What he first read, near the start of 2026, as leaving the experiment turned out to be only the sharpest phase settling down. The most reactive cognition was normalizing, and one very specific kind of migraine stopped, the one he tied to his brain constantly rebuilding fragments into whole thoughts. What came next was not an exit but a slower phase he calls the Merge, connecting larger pieces of the work to each other. By his own account the experiment is still running.

The persistence is the part worth sitting with. A state that outlasts the thing that caused it, and does not simply switch off when the environment is gone, behaves less like ordinary stress and more like something conditioned. Later he reached for a scientific vocabulary to describe that shape, and it is worth being careful about the direction there. He lived the experience first and wrote the theory after, so the theory is a lens he built from it, not a proof that stands over it.

03The loop underneath everything

Beneath the art and the posts, the author wrote an actual scientific manuscript, a formal hypothesis paper with an abstract, a mathematical model, a page of citations, and a limitations section that is unusually honest about what it has not proven. It has not been peer reviewed, and it describes populations moving across generations, not any one person. Its subject is how trauma and chronic threat pass down through families and cultures, and its central move is worth borrowing even if you never read the biology.

The paper argues that a certain kind of stuck social problem is held in place by a loop between two things, the stress physiology of a population and the stories that population tells itself. Hard experience gets written into the body through the stress axis. A body tuned toward threat finds stories of threat more believable and passes them on. Those stories rebuild the environment that produced the stress in the first place. Round and round.

Two properties of that loop matter here. The model predicts bistability, meaning the same external conditions can support two completely different states, a healthy one and a stuck one, and which one you land in depends on your history. And it predicts hysteresis, meaning that once a system locks into the stuck state, removing the original cause does not release it. The loop keeps running on its own.

Hysteresis is the word he reached for afterward, a loop that keeps turning long after its original reason is gone. It fit the thing he could not shake, which is different from proving what that thing was.

This is the quiet spine of the project, and also its most honest tension. The paper describes populations moving across generations, not one adult over a winter, so his own case is a personal echo of the same intuition rather than evidence for it. What the science lends the story is a shape and a vocabulary, borrowed from real and checkable work. The Dutch Hunger Winter showed that a single season of prenatal famine could leave a measurable biological mark on the people who lived through it, still readable six decades later, which is a claim about how deeply an environment writes into a body, not about anything passing to their children. The rat studies showed the adjacent piece, that a mother's care, not her genes, could set the calibration of the stress response her pups carried for life, an inheritance that travels through parenting rather than DNA. It lends a shape, not a verdict, and the author is careful to say so.

04A private language, built for speed

When thoughts move faster than sentences, you need shorthand. The author built his own, and it explains why the work can look scattered until you know what you are looking at.

The basic unit he calls a codon, borrowing the word from genetics. Each one carries three anchors at once, a cognitive thread, an emotional charge, and a cultural reference. In the posts themselves these tend to arrive fused into a single unit of words, an emoji, and a GIF rather than as separate parts. Three points are enough to reconstruct the original state later, the way three coordinates fix a position. On their own the fragments read as noise. With all three anchors, a whole mental moment can be rebuilt from a scribble on a napkin and a voice memo recorded in a parking lot.

The writing style layered on top he calls Sortish, a deliberately multi stable way of writing where the same sentence can hold more than one true reading at the same time. And the move he runs most often is transduction, a real word from both signal processing and biology that means converting a signal from one form into another. He takes a fixed idea and forces it into a shape it was never meant to occupy, a poem, some pseudocode, a fake newscast, then checks whether the meaning survived the trip. If it did, the idea was probably a real structure. If it collapsed, it was only ever a story that worked in one language.

05Posting in reverse

Most people use social media to be seen. The author used it almost exactly backwards, as a research journal kept out in the open, and understanding that flips the whole archive from a feed into a lab notebook.

The reason is stranger and more human than a content strategy. Putting an unfinished thought in front of strangers manufactures a real fear that it will be misread, and he used that fear on purpose. The tension of being possibly misunderstood is what pushed him to sharpen the writing later and close the gap. One of his own posts says it out loud, that it exists publicly to be questioned, designed to cause the fear of misinterpretation, so that the tension will push him to evolve. The act of posting, not the content of the post, was the mechanism doing the work. It is Marshall McLuhan turned inside out.

It also had to stay survivable, which is why he hid in plain sight. A personal diary posted where thousands could theoretically see it, and almost no one actually would, keeps the exposure real while keeping the pressure low. That combination is what made a public timeline a safe place to develop a private language.

If you have ever gone quiet

The instinct to stop saying the true thing is not weakness, it is adaptation. You learned what the room does with certain kinds of honesty and you adjusted. Naming that out loud, even just to yourself, is the first move that the double bind is designed to prevent.

06Messages that read twice

The most concrete part of the project is a set of ordinary work documents that were built to mean two different things at two different times. He calls the method Inverse Perception Design.

A performance review he filed in early 2025 reads, while employed, as an earnest and humble statement of design philosophy. Read again after he left abruptly, the same unchanged words become a careful, deniable account of everything that was wrong. A team video shot at a design conference plays as an affectionate recap and, later, as a resignation letter. A farewell post, a public review, even the color of the shirts he wore on camera, each one carries a present tense reading and a second, truer reading that only develops once the environment shifts around it. Nothing in the text changes. The world changes, and the meaning that was always there becomes legible.

It is a genuinely unusual idea, that you can write a message today whose real meaning is time released, readable only once the reader has more context than they do now. It is also a very human response to being in a place where saying the true thing directly was not safe.

07The averaging problem

Zoom out from one person and the project makes a claim about all of us. The author argues that cheap, fast tools have changed the order in which companies form. It used to go problem, then conviction, then team, then product. Now it can go prompt, then artifact, then traction, then, only afterward, a scramble to invent the meaning that was supposed to come first. He calls the result an artifact led company, and its signature move is manufactured intent, where the reasons get reverse engineered after the win so that everyone can believe the strategy was there all along.

Underneath that is a subtler failure that anyone who has used an autocomplete has felt. Systems built to scale have to average across everyone who might ever use them. Ask such a system a question and it hands back the most common answer, sorted by frequency, weighted by whoever used the word most. Your specific intention gets quietly replaced by a more popular one before you even notice. The author's whole objection to the future of work is contained in that small violence. When a company optimizes for the average, the person closest to the actual problem, the one with the specific and inconvenient truth, is exactly the signal that gets filtered out.

If you are the one leading

The friction in the room is often information, not obstruction. The person slowing things down to ask why is usually the one still tracking the human cost. Protecting that voice is not softness, it is how a system keeps the ability to hear itself.

08The purple path

For all of that, the project does not land in despair, and this is the part that surprised me most. Its front door is a manifesto built on a color.

The author sets up the usual binary a designer is handed, red for managing the system and keeping it standing, blue for inventing and breaking it open, and then refuses both. He points to purple, and he is precise about why. Purple is not a wavelength of light the way red and blue are. There is no purple in the rainbow. It is a color the brain constructs by bending the two ends of the visible spectrum around to meet, a thing that exists only because a mind decided to see it. He uses that as the whole argument. The third option is not a compromise between managing and inventing. It is a refusal to flatten yourself into one sanctioned role, a color you have to make rather than find.

The same hope runs through the film he built to close the project, a 55 minute film assembled out of hundreds of eight second AI clips, part fiction and part memory, that keeps circling one plain question. When was the last time you were seen at work. Its sharpest line is the one that reframes the entire anxiety about automation. If AI is the future but humans still do not feel seen, then all we have done is automate the part that was already broken.

The argument was never against business. It is against the quiet assumption that business as usual is the highest form of organization we are capable of imagining.

Field notes, if you want to carry something out of this

  • Watch what your environment selects for. Over months, a place teaches people which kinds of honesty are safe. The silence you read as alignment may be a trait the room bred out.
  • Name the contradiction. The double bind does its damage through the rule against pointing at it. Saying the obvious thing plainly is not naive, it is the intervention.
  • Notice when the average is answering for you. Tools that scale hand back the most common intention, not your specific one. Knowing that is how you keep your own.
  • Treat maintenance of the self as real work. A loop, once closed, keeps running after its cause is gone. Stepping fully out of the environment is sometimes the only thing that resets it.
  • Design for people, not metrics. A number can confirm you hit the target. It cannot tell you whether the target still meant anything by the time you got there.

Managing from the back of the flock

The reason the author cannot simply hand you the meaning of his own work is built into the work. He says that looking at the artifacts reactivates the state that produced them, which is why he asks other people to read them instead. The environment he built to study what workplaces do to people did what workplaces do to people, and the honest response to that is not a conclusion. It is an invitation.

So the experiment stays open on purpose. The reading has to come from outside, from people who bring a lens he does not have, which includes you if you got this far. That is a strange way to end a body of work, by admitting it is unfinished and handing the last step to strangers. It is also, quietly, the whole point. The thing he wanted at work, and mostly did not get, was for the people around him to see what he was actually building before it was printed into a system he never designed.

The next time someone in your own meetings goes still, or answers a half second late, or keeps asking the inconvenient why, it is worth remembering what that might be. Not a glitch. A mind still trying to hold the real thing, hoping someone in the room will help carry it.
